## How do Spark master and worker nodes coordinate job execution?

In a Spark standalone architecture, the coordination between the master and worker nodes follows a master-slave model where the master node acts as the central coordinator and the worker nodes serve as the execution environment.
The coordination of job execution happens through the following stages:

**1. Job Submission and Driver Initialization**

The process begins when a client submits a job (usually a JAR file) to the Spark Master. Upon submission, a Driver Program is launched. This driver is the central "brain" of the job, containing the main method or Spark Context that takes care of the entire execution.

**2. Resource Negotiation**

The Driver determines the specific resources required—such as the amount of RAM, CPU, and the number of tasks—needed to complete the job. It then communicates with the Cluster Manager (which resides on the Master in standalone mode) to request these resources.

**3. Launching and Registering Executors**

Once the Cluster Manager receives the request, it identifies which worker nodes have the necessary resources and starts Executors on those nodes.
• Data Locality: The Master tries to coordinate this based on "Data Locality," meaning it starts executors on worker nodes where the data blocks (e.g., Block 0, Block 1) are already stored to minimize data movement.
• Registration: Once executors are started on the worker nodes, they must register themselves with the Driver Program. This allows the Driver to take over the responsibility of monitoring the work.

**4. Task Distribution and Execution**

After registration, the Driver sends specific Tasks (which are JVM processes) to the executors.
• Parallelism: Each executor runs these tasks in parallel on different data blocks.
• Consolidation: If the job requires grouping or aggregating results (like calculating a "state topper" from "district results"), the Driver and Master coordinate to launch final tasks that collect and process the outputs from the initial parallel tasks.

**5. Continuous Monitoring (Heartbeats)**

The coordination is maintained through Heartbeat Communication.
• Executors and worker nodes constantly send heartbeats to the Driver and Master.
• This feedback loop allows the Driver to know if a node is still "live".
• If a heartbeat stops, the Master/Driver recognizes a failure and initiates recovery, such as re-launching an executor or re-running a failed task.

**6. Communication Constraints**

In this architecture, communication is strictly hierarchical. Worker nodes (slaves) do not communicate with each other regarding their health or resource status. They only share data with each other during a shuffle/transaction, but all health and status coordination must go through the Master.
If the Master itself needs coordination for high availability, ZooKeeper is used to manage an "Active Master" and "Passive Masters," ensuring that if the primary coordinator fails, a standby node can take over the coordination duties.
